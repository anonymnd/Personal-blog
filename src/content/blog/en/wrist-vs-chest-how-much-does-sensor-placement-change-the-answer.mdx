Every experiment on this blog so far has used scalp EEG. This one is a deliberate change of signal, to see whether the pattern I keep finding — pooling people inflates a score, evaluating properly brings it back down — shows up somewhere that isn't EEG at all.

<Callout type="key-insight" label="The question this answers">
If I switch from a clinical-grade chest sensor rig to the kind of wrist band someone might actually wear, how much accuracy does that cost — on the exact same people, the exact same task?
</Callout>

## The data

<SourceLink authors="Schmidt et al." year="2018" title="Introducing WESAD, a Multimodal Dataset for Wearable Stress and Affect Detection" href="https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3242969.3242985" /> recorded 15 people wearing two devices at once during a lab protocol with a stress-inducing task and a resting baseline: a RespiBAN chest unit (ECG, EDA, EMG, respiration, temperature, 3-axis accelerometer, all at 700 Hz) and an Empatica E4 wrist band (blood volume pulse at 64 Hz, EDA and temperature at 4 Hz, accelerometer at 32 Hz). Same person, same fifteen minutes, two different sensor placements.

I cut both signals into 30-second windows, kept only windows labeled entirely "baseline" or entirely "stress," and computed the mean and standard deviation of each channel per window — 12 features for chest, 8 for wrist. Same logistic regression, same subject-wise 5-fold evaluation (`GroupKFold` by subject) as the EEG experiments.

## The result

<tbody>
<tr><td>Chest (RespiBAN)</td><td>12</td><td>0.807 ± 0.066</td><td>0.500 ± 0.021</td></tr>
<tr><td>Wrist (Empatica E4)</td><td>8</td><td>0.694 ± 0.060</td><td>0.494 ± 0.010</td></tr>

Both devices clearly separate from chance — this isn't a case of one sensor working and the other not working at all. But the gap between them is real: 0.114 balanced accuracy, over 11 points, lost by going from the chest rig to the wrist band, on the same subject-wise evaluation that the last two posts used.

<Callout type="formula" label="Why the gap is believable">
The chest unit measures ECG and EMG directly against the skin near the heart and diaphragm — signals tied closely to the physiological stress response. The wrist band infers a lot of that indirectly, through blood volume pulse and skin conductance at a much noisier location. Losing signal quality by moving the sensor further from the source is exactly what you'd expect; the question was only ever how much.
</Callout>

## Why this matters for the "sense, interpret, adapt" loop

This connects back to the two posts before it, not just by reusing the same evaluation method. A wrist-worn device is the realistic form factor for anything that has to run outside a lab — which means any real adaptive system built on this kind of signal is choosing to pay this 11-point cost for comfort and practicality. Whether that tradeoff is worth it depends entirely on what the system needs to be right about, and that's a design question, not a modeling one.

<Callout type="warning" label="What this doesn't show">
Fifteen subjects is a small sample for a cost estimate like this, and the stress label here comes from a lab protocol (a timed public-speaking and arithmetic stress task), not from someone's real daily stress — so "11 points" is specific to this task, this population, and these two devices. It is a number from one experiment, not a general wrist-vs-chest constant.
</Callout>
